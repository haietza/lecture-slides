Agile Software Development

  • Before we talk about what Agile is, we need to understand what it was reacting to.
  • In the 1950s and 60s, use of computers and software development increased rapidly.
  • Problems arose due to the inadequacy of the existing methods to solve increasingly complex problems.
  • In 1968, the industry created the NATO conference to address these chronic and systemic problems.
  • Software development was modeled after civil engineering — you wouldn't pour concrete before the blueprints were done, right?
  • So Waterfall made intuitive sense. But software is fundamentally different from a bridge.

Why Does Agile Exist?

The Problem With Building Software Like a Bridge

  • 1968 NATO Conference: Software Crisis
    • Best practices for software
    • Acceptance of term software engineering
  • 1970 Waterfall (Royce)
    • Original process model
    • Linear and sequential: Requirements → Design → Code → Test → Deploy
    • Limited to no return to earlier phases
  • A bridge's requirements don't change after the foundation is poured.
  • Software requirements change constantly — the business learns something, the market shifts, the user tells you what they actually wanted.
  • Waterfall left no room for that. If you are in the testing phase and discover a fundamental flaw in the original analysis, Waterfall makes it incredibly slow and expensive to go backward.
  • By 2001, a critical mass of practitioners had enough evidence that something different was needed, and they wrote it down in four sentences.

The Agile Shift

  • The Catalyst: "Lightweight" process advocates met in Snowbird, Utah (2001).
  • The Focus: Addressing chronic, industry-wide deployment failures.
  • The Solution: Shifting focus from rigid structural compliance to an adaptive framework mindset.
  • This slide is frequently misunderstood.
  • The manifesto does NOT say 'processes and tools have no value.'
  • It says we value interactions more. That distinction matters enormously.
  • 'Working software over documentation' doesn't mean write no docs.
  • It means: if you're behind on a deadline, ship the working feature before writing the 40-page design document for it.
  • The right-side items still have value, they just lose the tie-breaker.

  • Welcome changing requirements, even late
  • Deliver working software frequently (prefer shorter timescales)

  • It actually means the right people talking directly rather than routing everything through email chains or tickets.

12 Principles

Team & Communication

  • Trust motivated individuals, give them support
  • Face-to-face conversation = most efficient and effective method
  • Best designs emerge from self-organizing teams
  • Principle 10 (maximizing the work not done) is counterintuitive.
  • In a traditional environment, more work means more value.
  • In agile, doing less work that's exactly right beats doing more work that's partially right.
  • That's lean thinking applied to software.

Engineering Excellence

  • Principle 11 (best architectures emerge from self-organizing teams) directly challenges the idea that one senior architect hands down a design.

Sustainability & Reflection

  • Reflect at regular intervals, tune accordingly

Three Layers, One System

  • Values: Abstract beliefs
  • Principles: Guiding rules
  • Practices: Concrete actions
  • When someone tells you 'we do daily standups,' the right question isn't 'is that the rule?'; it's 'what value and principle does this serve?'
  • If your team communicates constantly and is co-located, a mandatory daily standup might actually violate the principle it was designed to serve.
  • Practices disconnected from their values become cargo cult; you're doing the ritual without understanding the reason.
  • Strong engineers understand the why so they can adapt the how.
  • A persona (a fictional user profile) is a great example of a practice: it implements the principle of understanding users, which is rooted in the value of customer collaboration

  • We often talk about 'going Agile' as if it's a single thing, but there are actually dozens of agile methodologies.
  • The three you're most likely to encounter on the job are Scrum, XP, and Kanban.
  • The crucial framing is that none of these is universally better than the others; they solve different problems.
  • Scrum is the most common in enterprise software because it provides the most structure around planning and coordination: who is doing what, by when.
  • XP is laser-focused on code quality; it emerged because teams were shipping working software that was a nightmare to maintain.
  • Kanban came from manufacturing and is best suited for teams doing continuous, flow-based work; think ops, support, or maintenance teams where work arrives unpredictably.

Scrum Values

  • Courage to take on challenges and stand up for their viewpoint
  • Respect for each other and trust others to do their jobs
  • Openness to share what they are working on and confident to bring problems or obstacles to the team
  • Focus on the “Sprint Goal” and moving team toward it, one task at a time
  • Commitment to delivering most valuable product they can
  • The three principles are the philosophical engine: information must be visible before it can be inspected, and it must be inspected before it can be adapted.

Scrum Principles

  • Transparency: Process workflows must be visible to everyone involved.
  • Inspection: Regularly evaluating artifacts to detect unwanted issues.
  • Adaptation: Adjusting the process immediately when variances occur.
  • Daily scrum standups are a practice rooted in values openness and courage. The practice manifests or realizes the principles of transparency and inspection.

Values ↔ Principles ↔ Practices

  • Values are the foundation. They are the most intuitive and abstract.
  • Practices are specific, concrete actions we take. Practices should be “rooted” in some value(s).
  • Principles bridge the wide gap between values and practices. We need some “in-between guidance” on how to make a value an active practice.
  • Scrum's defining characteristic is tight project management structure — you always know what's being built, who owns what, and when every coordination point happens.
  • The 3-5-3 is the skeleton.
  • Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland emphasize that the Scrum framework is immutable. If you only implement bits and pieces, you aren't actually running Scrum.

The 3-5-3 of Scrum

  • 3 Roles: Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers
  • 5 Events: Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Retrospective
  • 3 Artifacts: Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment
  • The Product Owner manages the backlog, not the people.
  • The Scrum Master is a leader who serves; they remove impediments and protect the team (some teams rotate scrum master each sprint); they are not a project manager.
  • Developers are self-managing: no one assigns tasks to them.

Scrum Roles

  • One Cohesive Unit: Small team focused on one objective at a time, the Product Goal
  • Flat Architecture: No internal hierarchies or sub-teams
  • Product Owner: Accountable for maximizing product value and managing the Product Backlog
  • Scrum Master: Accountable for team effectiveness and establishing Scrum practices
  • Developers: Accountable for creating a usable, high-quality Increment each Sprint
  • Five events are all timeboxed.
  • The sprint serves as the baseline container for everything else.
  • The Sprint Planning's three topics matter: Why produces the Sprint Goal, What is selected based on velocity and capacity, and How belongs entirely to the Developers — no one dictates implementation.
  • The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute synchronization, not a status report to management.
  • The most common antipattern: the Scrum Master goes around the room collecting updates, turning it into a 40-minute meeting.
  • Surface blockers, identify who helps, solve it after.
  • The Definition of Done is what separates genuine completion from 'done on my machine' — a strong DoD includes peer review, passing tests, and at minimum a staging deployment.
  • The Review/Retro distinction trips students up on assessments: Review is external-facing, shows the product to stakeholders.
  • Retrospective is internal — how do we work better? It's the first event cut under pressure, which is exactly backwards.

Scrum Events

  • Sprint (1–4 weeks): Container event, fixed cadence
  • Sprint Planning (≤ 4 hrs): Why? / What? / How? (Devs own "How")
  • Daily Scrum (15 min): Sync toward Sprint Goal; surface blockers, not a status report
  • Sprint Review (≤ 2 hrs): Inspect the product with stakeholders
  • Sprint Retrospective (≤ 1.5 hrs): Inspect the process, team only
  • The product backlog is the long-term objective and functional target for the team.
  • The spring backlog is the single, focused objective for the active sprint.
  • The Increment is the formal quality bar that a feature must meet to be considered usable.
  • If a feature fails the DoD, it cannot be released or demoed, it goes straight back to the backlog.

  • User stories are a GASP (Generally Accepted Scrum Practice), not formally in the Scrum Guide but nearly universal in industry.
  • As a bank customer, I want to see the balances of each of my accounts, So that I can easily transfer money.
  • Story points are not time; a 5-point story means roughly 2.5× harder than a 2-point story, not 5 hours. Humans are bad at estimating absolute time but good at relative complexity.

User Stories

  • Structured templates to capture software requirements from an end-user perspective:
    • As a [type of user], I want [some goal or objective], so that [benefit, value].
  • Story Points: Relative effort (complexity + uncertainty + work):
    • Login page (simple, done before) = 2 pts
    • OAuth2 SSO (new, many unknowns) = 13 pts
  • Velocity: Points/sprint → forecasting: 100-pt backlog ÷ 20 pts/sprint ≈ 5 sprints
  • Planning Poker is another GASP (Generally Accepted Scrum Practice).
  • Planning Poker is typically conducted on the first day of a sprint during the team's formal planning session.
  • The team examines individual product backlog items presented.
  • The developers discuss the architectural requirements of a story with the Product Owner, vote on the relative effort (story points) using independent cards, and discuss to adjust their perspectives as a group.
  • The Fibonacci sequence's growing gaps mathematically represent the exponential growth of risk and uncertainty as a feature gets larger.
  • It also stops false precision, wasting time arguing over meaningless intervals (11-hour task vs 12-hour task).
  • It helps surface differing design assumptions before writing any code, ensuring the entire team shares a clear, unified understanding of the technical requirements.
  • The simultaneous card flip in Planning Poker is an epistemic tool: if your senior dev plays 2 and your junior plays 13, that gap contains information. The conversation that follows closes the knowledge gap for the whole team.
  • The process repeats until the developers converge on an agreed-upon estimate, establishing the total effort required to build out that specific user story.

Planning Poker

  • Purpose: To collaboratively estimate the total effort needed to deliver a specific user story
  • Mechanics: Uses a relative scale (often Fibonacci values) to align estimates across the group
  • Value: Avoids early bias, highlights design misunderstandings, and taps into the collective intelligence of the developers
  • XP answers a completely different question from Scrum.
  • Scrum asks: how do we coordinate what work gets done? XP asks: how do we write software that won't collapse under its own weight?
  • Notice the values — Courage and Respect are also Scrum values.
  • Communication addresses the root cause of most engineering failures.

XP Values

  • Courage: Take on challenges, stand up for your viewpoint
  • Respect: Trust teammates, they trust you
  • Communication: Most problems trace back to a communication failure
  • Feedback: Change is inevitable, feedback tells you something changed
  • Simplicity: Maximize the work not done, manage complexity
  • The 13 practices fall into three groups.
  • Pair Programming: two devs, one keyboard (Driver + Navigator; Driver types, Navigator reviews, thinks ahead, catches errors live; spreads knowledge, reduces single points of failure)
  • Test-First Programming (TDD): write the test before the code (forces interface design before implementation)
  • Incremental Design" design evolves; don't over-architect upfront
  • The team practices are about removing friction: sit together, make workspace information visible, protect the team's energy.
  • Project management in XP is lightweight by design because the engineering discipline is doing the heavy lifting.
  • Ten-Minute Build: If tests take > 10 min, developers stop running them.
  • Continuous integration: Integrate to the shared branch multiple times per day; each commit triggers automated build + full test suite; catch conflicts and regressions in minutes, not days
  • The coding practices are the core of XP's influence on modern software engineering; TDD and CI are now considered standard practice far beyond teams that formally adopt XP.

  • Kanban comes from Toyota's manufacturing system.
  • A pull system where work is pulled to the next stage only when capacity exists, rather than pushed regardless of readiness.
  • The central principle is visibility: put all work on a board so everyone can see the state of everything.
  • The WIP (work in progress) limit is the most powerful practice.
  • Say your WIP limit on Development is 3 and all slots are full. In most teams, developers pick up more work anyway.
  • In Kanban, that's not allowed — the limit forces the question: why isn't work flowing? Is something blocked? Is there a testing bottleneck? Is someone context-switching?
  • The WIP limit makes invisible problems visible.
  • Kanban has no sprints, no fixed roles, no planning ceremonies. Work arrives continuously and flows through the board.
  • This suits support, operations, or maintenance work where you can't predict what's coming next.
  • Caution “procrastination” is not the same thing as decide as late as possible!

Kanban Principles

  • Eliminate Waste: Remove anything that doesn't add value.
  • Amplify Learning: Use feedback loops continuously.
  • Decide as Late as Possible: Most information exists at the last responsible moment.
  • Deliver as Fast as Possible: Reduce cycle time; don't batch unnecessarily.
  • Empower the Team: Those closest to the work make the best decisions.
  • Build Integrity In: Quality is not a phase; it's continuous.
  • See the Whole: Optimize the system, not just individual steps.
  • The MOST well known aspect of Kanban. Built into lots of tools including GitHub (as GitHub “projects”).
  • Both Analysis and Development are at their WIP limits — the amber WIP 2/2 and WIP 3/3 badges signal that no new work should enter those columns until something exits.
  • Testing has capacity at 1/2, which is the correct visual cue that work should be pulled there first.
  • Backlog and Done have no limits, which is standard practice.

Kanban: Flow Metrics

Metric Measures Use to...
Cycle Time "Started" → "Done" Predict delivery reliability
Lead Time "Requested" → "Done" Set customer expectations
Flow Efficiency % of time actively worked vs. waiting Expose waste
  • Value Stream Mapping makes this visible by tracing the full journey and marking every wait.
  • Once you see that a feature spends 3 days waiting for code review in a 10-day cycle, you have a concrete, measurable target.
  • This is a fundamentally different improvement model from Scrum's retrospective.
  • Scrum builds in a structured reflection at the end of every sprint.
  • Kanban treats improvement as continuous and metrics-driven — change one thing, measure the effect.
  • Neither is better. Scrum's cadence suits teams that need regular forcing functions.
  • Kanban's continuous model suits teams where improvement opportunities emerge unpredictably.

  • The key insight is that the best teams treat these as a toolkit, not laws.
  • The three-layer model from Class 1 Segment 1 is your evaluation framework for any practice you encounter.
  • When someone tells you 'we do planning poker' or 'we have WIP limits' — the right question is what principle does this serve and what value is it rooted in?
  • If you can answer that, you can decide whether it fits your context. If you can't, you're doing cargo cult agile.
  • The ecosystem effect is real: practices from different methodologies reinforce each other.
  • Scrum gives you the cadence and coordination.
  • XP gives you the engineering discipline to produce quality within that cadence.
  • Kanban gives you the flow metrics to see what's actually happening.
  • These aren't competing frameworks — they're complementary lenses on the same problem.
  • Don't expect a silver bullet to solve all our problems
  • Agile practices are currently considered "best practices", but best practices do change.

Blending Methodologies
